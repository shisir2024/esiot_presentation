1. Introduction & Fundamentals

Definition: A low pass filter (LPF) is a circuit that passes low‑frequency signals from DC up to a cut‑off frequency fc while attenuating higher frequencies. In embedded systems, LPFs are used for anti‑aliasing, noise reduction, and sensor signal conditioning.

Operating principle: A first‑order RC LPF uses a resistor and capacitor. The capacitor’s reactance XC = 1/(2πfC) decreases with frequency, effectively short‑circuiting high‑frequency components to ground, while low frequencies pass with minimal attenuation.

Importance in ECE: LPFs are ubiquitous in analog and mixed‑signal design, communication receivers, power supplies, and embedded sensor interfaces. They form the building block for more complex active filters.

Prerequisites: Basic circuit laws (KVL/KCL), phasors, complex impedance, transfer functions, and decibel notation.

Key terminology: Cut‑off frequency (−3 dB point), passband, stopband, roll‑off (−20 dB/decade), time constant (τ = RC), phase shift.

3. Working Principle & Detailed Explanation

DC Behaviour (f = 0)

At DC, the capacitor behaves as an open circuit. No current flows through C, so no voltage drop across R (assuming no load). Therefore Vout = Vin; the filter passes DC unchanged.

AC Behaviour (f > 0)

As frequency increases, the capacitive reactance XC = 1/(ωC) decreases. The capacitor increasingly shunts the signal to ground, reducing the output amplitude. At very high frequencies, XC → 0, so Vout → 0.

Cut‑off frequency

Defined as the frequency where |Vout| = Vin/√2 (−3 dB) and the phase shift is −45°. For the RC LPF:

fc = 1 / (2π R C)

Time constant: τ = RC. Also, fc = 1/(2πτ).

Role of components

R and C form a frequency‑dependent voltage divider. The output is taken across C. At low frequencies, C has high impedance → most voltage appears across C. At high frequencies, C has low impedance → output is shorted.

Advantages & limitations

Advantages: Simple, passive, low cost, no power supply. Limitations: Gentle roll‑off (−20 dB/decade), loading effects, no gain.

Common misconceptions

  • “The capacitor blocks DC” – false; it blocks changes in voltage, but steady DC passes (after transient).
  • “Output is zero at high frequency” – practically, parasitic elements cause a small feedthrough.
  • “Cut‑off is where gain is 0.707 of maximum” – correct only for first‑order filters.

5. Mathematical Equations & Derivations

Transfer function H(s):

H(s) = Vout(s) / Vin(s) = (1/(sC)) / (R + 1/(sC)) = 1 / (1 + s R C)

Magnitude and phase (s = jω):

|H(jω)| = 1 / √(1 + (ωRC)²)
∠H(jω) = − arctan(ωRC)

Cut‑off frequency (ωc = 1/RC):

fc = 1 / (2π R C)

At f = fc : |H| = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 (−3 dB), phase = −45°.

Roll‑off: −20 dB/decade for f ≫ fc.

Time constant: τ = RC, step response: vout(t) = Vin(1 − e−t/τ).

6. Solved Numerical Problems

Example 1 (Easy)

Q: An RC LPF has R = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF. Find the cut‑off frequency.

Solution: fc = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 104 × 10−8) = 1/(2π × 10−4) ≈ 1591.5 Hz.

Interpretation: Signals above ~1.6 kHz are attenuated.

Example 2 (Moderate)

Q: For the above filter, find the output voltage magnitude at f = 5 kHz if input is 2 V peak.

Solution: ω = 2π×5000 = 31416 rad/s. ωRC = 31416 × 104 × 10−8 = 3.1416. |H| = 1/√(1+3.1416²) = 1/√(1+9.87) = 1/3.30 = 0.303. Vout = 0.303×2 = 0.606 V peak.

Interpretation: At 5 kHz, the signal is attenuated to ~30% of input.

Example 3 (GATE‑style, Difficult)

Q: A first‑order RC LPF has a −3 dB frequency of 2 kHz. If the resistor is 4.7 kΩ, determine the capacitor value and the attenuation (in dB) at 20 kHz.

Solution: C = 1/(2π fc R) = 1/(2π×2000×4700) = 1/(5.9×107) ≈ 16.9 nF. At 20 kHz: f/fc = 10. |H| = 1/√(1+100) ≈ 0.0995 → −20.04 dB.

Interpretation: One decade above cut‑off, attenuation ≈ −20 dB as expected.

8. Core Electronics Company Interview Preparation

Q1. Why is a capacitor used in a low pass filter and not an inductor?

Answer: Capacitors are smaller, cheaper, and more ideal at low frequencies. Inductors are bulkier, have series resistance, and can cause EMI. For RC filters, the capacitor’s impedance decreases with frequency, providing a simple shunt path to ground.

Q2. How would you design an anti‑aliasing filter for an ADC sampling at 10 kSps?

Answer: Set the cut‑off frequency below half the sampling rate (Nyquist), e.g., fc ≈ 4–5 kHz. Choose R and C to achieve that fc, and ensure the filter attenuates frequencies above Nyquist sufficiently (e.g., −40 dB). Often a higher‑order filter is needed.

Q3. What is the effect of loading on an RC LPF?

Answer: If a load resistance is connected across C, it forms a parallel combination and alters the effective cut‑off frequency and DC gain. The output impedance of the source also adds to R. Always buffer with an op‑amp if needed.

Q4. Explain the step response of an RC LPF.

Answer: It is an exponential rise: vout(t)=Vin(1−e−t/τ). The 10–90% rise time ≈ 2.2τ. This is critical for sensor interface timing.

Q5. How does a low pass filter reduce noise in embedded systems?

Answer: It attenuates high‑frequency noise (e.g., from switching regulators or digital clocks) that couples into analog signal lines. Placed close to the ADC input, it prevents aliasing and improves SNR.

Q6. What is the difference between active and passive low pass filters?

Answer: Passive uses only R, L, C; no power, but cannot provide gain and suffers from loading. Active uses op‑amps, provides gain, buffering, and allows higher Q without inductors.

Q7. How would you measure the cut‑off frequency in the lab?

Answer: Apply a sine sweep and measure the output amplitude. Find the frequency where output is 0.707×Vin (or −3 dB). Use an oscilloscope or network analyser.

Q8. What is the phase shift at fc and why does it matter?

Answer: −45°. It matters in feedback loops and communication systems because phase delay can affect stability and signal integrity.

Q9. How does temperature affect an RC LPF?

Answer: Resistor and capacitor values drift with temperature (temperature coefficient). This shifts fc. Use precision components (C0G, metal film) for critical applications.

Q10. What is a common mistake when simulating an RC LPF in Multisim?

Answer: Forgetting to set the AC analysis correctly, or not adding a ground reference. Also, ideal components may hide real‑world parasitic effects.

Industry perspective: Low pass filters are used in every mixed‑signal IC, sensor interface, and power supply. Engineers must balance cut‑off, roll‑off, cost, and board space.

9. Applications & Industry Relevance

  • Anti‑aliasing filters before ADC in embedded systems.
  • Noise reduction in audio, sensor, and instrumentation amplifiers.
  • Power supply filtering to smooth rectified AC.
  • Demodulation in communication receivers.
  • Button debouncing in digital interfaces.

Challenges: Component tolerance, parasitic effects, and loading. Career relevance: Analog design, embedded hardware, validation engineer.

Quick Revision Points

  • At f = fc, gain = 0.707, phase = −45°.
  • Passband: f ≪ fc → gain ≈ 1 (0 dB).
  • Stopband: f ≫ fc → gain decreases by 20 dB/decade.
  • Used for anti‑aliasing, noise reduction, and smoothing.
  • Active filters overcome loading and provide gain.

Common errors

  • Confusing cut‑off with unity gain frequency.
  • Ignoring source/load impedance.
  • Forgetting that capacitor blocks DC only in steady state.
